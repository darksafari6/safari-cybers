## Evidence before conclusions

One office reports that the portal name fails to resolve. Another office opens it successfully. You have ten minutes to give the service owner a useful update. The tempting answer is “office A has a broken resolver.” A better answer is a short comparison that another person can repeat.

### Start with a fair comparison

Compare the same name, close in time, and write down the resolver used by each client. Include the result rather than just “worked” or “failed.” A resolver that returns a negative answer is behaving differently from one that never responds. If you compare two checks separated by an hour, you may be comparing after a recovery rather than across offices.

```text
Office A | 09:04 UTC | portal.example | resolver A | no answer before timeout
Office B | 09:05 UTC | portal.example | resolver B | address returned
```

This fictional log supports the statement that two lookup attempts had different results. It does not establish that resolver A is universally unavailable. In a real incident, capture scope carefully: devices, networks, time window, and which user workflows were affected. Preserve the original observations rather than rewriting them to fit your theory.

### Make competing explanations earn their place

**Hypothesis 1: the office’s configured resolver is unavailable.** If several unrelated names also time out using the same resolver, confidence rises. If most names resolve normally and only the portal fails, a blanket outage becomes less plausible.

**Hypothesis 2: the portal is affected by a name-specific problem.** That could explain why other lookups work. Compare answers for the exact name and document whether a result was cached. An old cached result on one device might make the service appear healthy even if new lookups are failing.

Avoid changing several settings at once. If you change the resolver, refresh a cache, and restart a service together, a successful result will not tell you which change mattered. When safe, observe first and change one variable at a time. When customer impact requires quick action, state what trade-off you made and keep a timestamped record.

### Worked handoff

> **Impact:** Portal name lookups timed out from two office A devices between 09:04 and 09:08 UTC; office B could resolve the same name. **Unknown:** Whether this affects all names or only the portal. **Next check:** Compare a known-good name using the same resolver, then update the incident channel by 09:15 UTC.

This handoff is deliberately modest. It is specific enough for the next person to reproduce, and it does not accidentally direct a service owner to restart a healthy application.

### Checkpoint: pick the better follow-up

The portal works again at 09:20. Do you close the ticket or record what changed? A healthy check shows the symptom is gone for that client at that instant. Confirm recovery across the affected scope, record what happened, and avoid claiming a cause that remains unverified.

### Take it into practice

Write an evidence table with columns for observation, possible explanation, and next check. Add a final line naming evidence that would make you abandon your favorite explanation. A hypothesis you cannot imagine disproving is not yet useful for triage.
