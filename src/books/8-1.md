## Timeline and confidence: checkout failed at 09:03

At 09:00, a fictional online shop releases a checkout update. Three minutes later, customers report errors. The product catalog still loads. Support says the whole site is down; engineering suspects the payment dependency. Your first task is to write down what the data actually supports.

- **09:00 UTC:** The release reached a subset of instances.
- **09:03 UTC:** Checkout errors increased; product browsing remained healthy.
- **09:06 UTC:** The payment provider’s public status was normal.
- **09:10 UTC:** Updated instances showed more checkout errors than unchanged instances.

### Compare groups carefully

Suppose updated instances handled 200 requests with 24 failures (12%) and unchanged instances handled 600 with 6 failures (1%). The difference is useful, but ask whether both groups served comparable traffic, regions, and user actions. A higher error rate on the updated group is evidence of association, not proof of a specific code defect. A public uptime page does not establish that every customer's integration is healthy.

### State a claim another person can check

> Checkout error rates rose on the updated group at 09:03 UTC. We are checking whether that difference holds across comparable traffic. Browsing remains available. The cause and full scope are not yet confirmed.

On a second incident, imagine both the updated and unchanged groups start failing at the same moment and share a downstream dependency. The deployment may still be worth reviewing, but it no longer cleanly separates affected and unaffected requests. Be willing to lower your confidence when the comparison changes.

### Checkpoint

Which fact would raise confidence that the release is related? A controlled comparison showing the difference persists after matching similar traffic, or a reversible rollback followed by improvement in the same group, would be stronger than timing alone.
